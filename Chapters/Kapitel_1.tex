\subsection{Measurement A: Rise- and fall time of the line driver}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The objective of this task is to measure the rise and fall times of a signal generator and the open output of a line driver. The line driver (LM5134 and LMG1025) is supplied with +5 V, and the function generator is set to output a rectangular signal with 5 V$_{\text{pp}}$ and a 2.5 V DC offset.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
The measurement configuration consists of an Arbitrary Waveform Generator connected to the input of the line driver. The output of the driver is connected to an SMA connector via two 2.7 $\Omega$ series resistors, providing a total of 5.4 $\Omega$ series resistance. The voltage is detected using a 1 k$\Omega$ resistor connected to a second SMA connector, which alongside the 50 $\Omega$ input of the oscilloscope and a 50 $\Omega$ coaxial transmission line, forms a 1:21 voltage divider.For Measurement A, the output coaxial line and load $\underline{Z}_L$ are left disconnected (open driver output) as shown in Figure 2.1.

\subsubsection{Short discussion of measurement results}

The measurements demonstrate a significant decrease in the rise time when the signal is passed through the line driver. The signal generator alone exhibits a relatively slow rise time of 8.354 ns and a fall time of 4.374 ns. After passing through the LM5134 and LMG1025 gate drivers, the rising edge is sharpened considerably to a rise time of 1.282 ns. This fast transition is essential for Time Domain Reflectometry, as a steeper edge allows for higher spatial resolution when distinguishing incident waves from reflected waves on the transmission line.

It must be noted that the fall time for the line driver could not be captured. The input signal was a $\sim$1 kHz rectangular wave, meaning the falling edge occurred approximately 500 $\mu$s after the rising edge. Because the oscilloscope timebase was set to a very fine resolution of 2.00 ns/div to accurately measure the nanosecond rise time, the subsequent falling edge was completely off-screen and not captured within the instrument's acquisition memory for this specific measurement. The overall measurement uncertainty for the captured rise times is primarily dictated by the sampling rate and the horizontal cursor resolution of the oscilloscope.

\subsection{Measurement B: Reflections of a coax cable with different loads}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
An RG142 coaxial cable is connected to the line driver output. The task is to terminate the end of the cable with different loads (Open, Short, 50 $\Omega$, 100 $\Omega$, 25 $\Omega$, 100 pF capacitor, and 1 nH inductor) and measure the resulting line reflections. The transient voltage curve is recorded to determine the time delay between reflection minima, the maximum voltage, and the steady-state asymptotic voltage. Using the measured propagation delay and the physical cable length, the relative dielectric constant ($\epsilon_r$) of the cable must be calculated.

The measurement arrangement is based on Figure 2.1, where an RG142 coaxial cable ($l = 2.0\text{ m}$) is attached to the driver output and terminated with various load impedances $\underline{Z}_L$ (Open, Short, 50 $\Omega$, 100 $\Omega$, 25 $\Omega$, 100 pF, 1 nH).

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The reflection factor $\rho_0$ at the load is determined by the load impedance $Z_L$ and the characteristic impedance $Z_w$ of the transmission line:

\begin{equation}
    \rho_0 = \frac{Z_L - Z_w}{Z_L + Z_w}
\end{equation}

The phase velocity $v_p$ of the signal propagating through the transmission line depends on the speed of light in a vacuum ($c = 2.9979 \times 10^8$ m/s) and the relative dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$:

\begin{equation}
    v_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}
\end{equation}

The time delay $t$ measured between the incident wave and the reflected wave represents the round trip of the signal along the length of the cable $l$:

\begin{equation}
    t = \frac{2l}{v_p}
\end{equation}

By substituting the phase velocity formula into the time delay formula, the dielectric constant $\epsilon_r$ can be calculated from the physical length and the measured time delay:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_r = \left( \frac{c \cdot t}{2l} \right)^2
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the "calculated" columns of the measurement table, the 100 $\Omega$ load and Open circuit measurements are evaluated below.

For a characteristic impedance of $Z_w = 50\ \Omega$ and a load of $Z_L = 100\ \Omega$, the reflection factor is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \rho_0 = \frac{100\ \Omega - 50\ \Omega}{100\ \Omega + 50\ \Omega} = \frac{50}{150} = +0.33
\end{equation}

For the Open load measurement, a time delay of $t = 19.6$ ns was recorded across the $l = 2.0$ m cable. The relative dielectric constant is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_r = \left( \frac{2.9979 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s} \cdot 19.6 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s}}{2 \cdot 2.0\ \text{m}} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{5.875}{4.0} \right)^2 \approx 2.16
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Short discussion of measurement results}

The measured transient responses align well with standard transmission line theory. The incident voltage step ($V_{\text{inc}}$) was estimated visually at 150 mV on the oscilloscope, which corresponds to a 3.15 V line voltage before the 1:21 voltage divider.

The behaviour of the specific loads can be summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open Circuit:} The reflection factor is +1. This positive reflection doubled the incident voltage, reaching a maximum of 300 mV.
    \item \textbf{Short Circuit:} The reflection factor is -1. The negative reflection canceled the voltage at the load, dropping it back to 0 mV.
    \item \textbf{Matched Load (50 $\Omega$):} The reflection factor is 0. No backward wave was generated, and the voltage remained constant at 150 mV.
    \item \textbf{100 $\Omega$ and 25 $\Omega$ Loads:} Both loads showed the expected partial reflections. The 100 $\Omega$ load created a positive reflection (+50 mV), settling at a steady state of 200 mV. The 25 $\Omega$ load created a negative reflection (-50 mV), settling at 100 mV.
    \item \textbf{100 pF Capacitor:} Initially acted as a short circuit upon the signal step, before charging up towards an open circuit steady-state ($\rho_0 \rightarrow +1$).
    \item \textbf{1 nH Inductor:} Initially resisted the current change and acted as an open circuit (producing a positive 300 mV peak), before settling into a short circuit steady-state ($\rho_0 \rightarrow -1$).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Time Delay and Dielectric Constant}

The time delay across the purely resistive, open, and short loads was highly consistent, averaging 19.55 ns.

Based on the 2.0 m length of the RG142 cable, the calculated average relative dielectric constant ($\epsilon_r$) is 2.15. This perfectly matches the theoretical permittivity for the cable's PTFE dielectric ($\epsilon_r \approx 2.1$).

The 1 nH inductor showed a minor variance in the time delay measurement ($\Delta X = 21.3$ ns). This is because it is much more difficult to accurately place cursors on a rounded, transient slope than on a sharp resistive edge.

\subsection{Measurement C: Determine cable length}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The goal is to determine the unknown physical length of a provided RG58 coaxial cable. This is achieved using Time Domain Reflectometry by measuring the propagation delay of a reflection and utilizing the relative dielectric constant ($\epsilon_r$) determined in Measurement B, as strictly instructed by the laboratory manual.

The setup remains as shown in Figure 2.1, with the RG142 cable replaced by an open-ended RG58 coaxial cable of unknown length

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

Using the time delay $t$ measured from the oscilloscope and the phase velocity $v_p$ (derived from the $\epsilon_r$ calculated in Measurement B), the unknown length $l$ of the cable can be calculated by rearranging the time delay formula:

\begin{equation}
    l = \frac{v_p \cdot t}{2} = \frac{c \cdot t}{2\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the value populated in the "calculated" column of the measurement table, the length of the unknown RG58 cable is evaluated below.

Given the speed of light in a vacuum $c = 2.9979 \times 10^8$ m/s, the adopted average relative dielectric constant $\epsilon_r = 2.15$ (from Measurement B), and the measured time delay $t = 12.0$ ns, the physical length $l$ of the cable is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    l = \frac{2.9979 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s} \cdot 12.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s}}{2\sqrt{2.15}} = \frac{3.5975\ \text{m}}{2 \cdot 1.466} \approx 1.23\ \text{m}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Short discussion of measurement results}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A positive reflection was captured on the oscilloscope, confirming the unknown RG58 cable was open at its far end. The round-trip time delay was measured at 12.0 ns using the cursors.
    \item Following the laboratory manual's instructions, the relative dielectric constant from Measurement B ($\epsilon_r = 2.15$) was used for the calculation. This resulted in a calculated cable length of 1.23 m.
    \item The actual physical length of the cable was measured to be exactly 1.20 m. The calculated value overestimates the true length by 3 cm.
    \item This deviation is a systematic error directly caused by using the material properties of the RG142 cable (PTFE dielectric) for an RG58 cable (polyethylene dielectric).
    \item Standard RG58 cables have a higher theoretical relative permittivity of $\epsilon_r = 2.26$. If this correct material constant is used in the formula instead of 2.15, the calculated length resolves to exactly 1.196 m. This is only 4 mm away from the 1.20 m physical measurement, proving the theoretical accuracy of the measurement.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Formulas and calculations}
\subsubsection{\textbf{Calculation of the group characteristic and the total radiation pattern:}}
Foremostly, the known specifications are:
\begin{gather}
	d = 62.5\mathrm{mm}	\nonumber\\
	\lambda=125\mathrm{mm} \nonumber\\
\end{gather}

where\begin{itemize}
	\item d represents the distance between 2 adjacent antennas in the array.
	\item $\lambda$ represents the wavelength of the RF signal.
\end{itemize}
Since there are 4 antennas, and each is sourced with equal phase:
\begin{gather}
	\delta_0=0 \nonumber \\
	N=4	\nonumber
\end{gather}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Using \cite{lec-script} [page 72], one can represent the array/group characteristic in such a form:
	\begin{gather}
		G(\varphi,\vartheta) = \frac{\sin(uN)}{N\sin(u)} \label{group_char}
	\end{gather}
	
	\item The value of '$u$' must be calculated now.\\ From \cite{lec-script} [page 73], one can deduce:
	\begin{gather}
		u = \frac{\delta_0}{2} - \pi\cdot\frac{d}{\lambda}\cdot\mathrm{cos}\varphi\cdot\mathrm{sin}\vartheta \nonumber \\
		\mathrm{where,}\quad
		\frac{d}{\lambda}=\frac{62.5}{125}=0.5	\nonumber \\
		\boxed{u = - 0.5\pi\cdot\mathrm{cos}\varphi\cdot\mathrm{sin}\vartheta} \label{value_u}
	\end{gather}

	\item This equation is set to meet the specifications of horizontally and vertically polarized antenna arrays:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For the horizontal polarisation, the $\vartheta$ is set to 90$^\circ$, which yields:
			\begin{gather}
				sin(\vartheta) = 1 \nonumber \\
				u = -\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\mathrm{cos}\varphi \\
				\varphi \in \mathrm{(0,360)} \nonumber
			\end{gather}

		\item For the vertical polarisation, the $\varphi$ can be set to 0$^\circ$, which yields:
			\begin{gather}
				cos(\varphi) = 1 \nonumber \\
				u = -\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\mathrm{sin}\vartheta \\
				\vartheta \in \mathrm{(0,360)} \nonumber
			\end{gather}
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item Following is the calculation for the total radiation pattern:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item One can assume the single dipole element factor to be at a constant 1 for the horizontal polarisation, this gives the following computation of the total radiation pattern equation found in \cite{lec-script}:
		\begin{gather}
			F_{\mathrm{tot}} = F(\varphi, \vartheta)\cdot G(\varphi, \vartheta) = 1\cdot G(\varphi, \vartheta) \nonumber \\
			\boxed {F_{\mathrm{tot-horz}} = G(\varphi, \vartheta)}			\label{horizontal_pattern}
		\end{gather}
		The resultant array characteristic has been plotted in \ref{fig:xscreen7}.
		\item However, for vertical polarization, this does not stay constant and changes with the angle, so one must calculate the angle-dependent dipole pattern F($\vartheta$), this gives:
		\begin{gather}
			F_{\mathrm{tot}} = F(\varphi, \vartheta)\cdot G(\varphi, \vartheta) \nonumber \\
			{F_{\mathrm{tot}} = \frac{\cos(\beta h \cos \vartheta)-\cos(\beta h)}{\sin \vartheta} \cdot \frac{\mathrm{sin}(Nu)}{N\mathrm{sin}(u)}} \nonumber \\
			\boxed {F_{\mathrm{tot-vert}} = \frac{\cos(\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\cos{\vartheta})}{\sin \vartheta} \cdot G(\varphi, \vartheta)} \label{vertical_pattern}
		\end{gather}
		The resultant array characteristic has been plotted in \ref{fig:xscreen8}.
	\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Calculation for Gain of the group compared to an intrinsic radiator:}}
To determine the total gain of the 4-antenna group compared to an intrinsic (isotropic) radiator, both theoretical array principles and relative laboratory measurements must be evaluated.

\paragraph{Theoretical Gain via Array Factor:}
An intrinsic radiator is defined as an isotropic source with a theoretical gain of $G_{\mathrm{iso}} = 1$ ($0\mathrm{\ dBi}$). 

For a single standard half-wave dipole, the theoretical gain is approximately:
\begin{gather}
	G_{\mathrm{single}} \approx 1.64 \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad 2.15\mathrm{\ dBi} \nonumber
\end{gather}

When $N$ identical antennas are arranged in a linear array and fed with equal phase, the radiation fields add up in the main beam direction. This increases the effective current from $I_0$ to $N \cdot I_0$, resulting in a power gain factor of $N$. For the used 4-antenna array ($N=4$), the theoretical group gain is, according to \cite{lec-script}:
\begin{gather}
	G_{\mathrm{group\_theory}} = N \cdot G_{\mathrm{single}} \nonumber \\
	G_{\mathrm{group\_theory}} = 4 \cdot 1.64 = 6.56 \label{eq:gain_linear}
\end{gather}

Expressed in decibels relative to an isotropic radiator (dBi):
\begin{gather}
	G_{\mathrm{group\_theory}}[\mathrm{dBi}] = 10\log_{10}(6.56) \nonumber \\
	\boxed{G_{\mathrm{group\_theory}} \approx 8.17\mathrm{\ dBi}} \label{eq:gain_db}
\end{gather}
This theoretical calculation indicates the group gain should be about $6\mathrm{\ dB}$ higher than the gain of a single half-wave dipole.\\

If the horn antenna gain was known, the gain of the antenna group could be estimated by comparing the measured received powers of both antennas under the same measurement conditions, at 2.4GHz:
\begin{gather}
	G_{\mathrm{array}}[\mathrm{dBi}] = G_{\mathrm{horn}}[\mathrm{dBi}] + \left(P_{\mathrm{array}} - P_{\mathrm{horn}}\right) \label{eq:array_gain}
\end{gather}

However, since the measurements were not made at 2.4GHz for the Horn antenna, the equation was not computed further.

\end{gather}

\end{gather}
By comparing $G_{\mathrm{group\_measured}}$ to the theoretical $8.17\mathrm{\ dBi}$, the efficiency and inherent losses of the multi-antenna setup can be quantified.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Calculation of the directivity of the group:}}
Directivity indicates the power increase in the main beam direction compared to the average
power radiated in all directions \cite{lec-script}.\\
The calculation of directivity for the horizontally polarised antenna array is:
\begin{gather}
	D_{\mathrm{horz}} = \frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{max}}}{\Phi_i}	\nonumber \\
	\mathrm{where,\quad} 	\nonumber \\
	\Phi_{\mathrm{i-norm}} = 0.2281 \mathrm{\quad and \quad} \Phi_{\mathrm{max-norm}} = 1 	\nonumber \\
	\boxed{D_{\mathrm{horz}} = \frac{1}{0.2281} = 4.284}
\end{gather}

The calculation of directivity for the vertically polarised antenna array is:
\begin{gather}
	D_{\mathrm{vert}} = \frac{\Phi_{\mathrm{max}}}{\Phi_i}	\nonumber \\
	\mathrm{where,\quad} 	\nonumber \\
	\Phi_{\mathrm{i-norm}} = 0.2152 \mathrm{\quad and \quad} \Phi_{\mathrm{max-norm}} = 1 	\nonumber \\
	\boxed{D_{\mathrm{vert}} = \frac{1}{0.2152} = 4.647}
\end{gather}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Calculation of the distance dependence of the received power:}}

For plotting the distance dependence of the received power one must understand the distance dependence of the received power. \\
According to the theory \cite{lec-script}, the received power in the far field follows the inverse-square law. Meaning that the electromagnetic field strength decreases with $1/r$, while the radiated power decreases with $1/r^2$.

\paragraph{Calculation formula:}
The received power can be (using \cite{friis-script}) calculated as:
\begin{gather}
	P_r = P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r \cdot \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi r}\right)^2 \label{eq:friis}
\end{gather}

where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $P_t$ is the transmitted power, set at +10dBm
	\item $G_t$ is the gain of the transmitting antenna, theoretical assumption of 6.56 = 8.17dBi
	\item $G_r$ is the gain of the receiving antenna, theoretical assumption of 1.64 = 2.15dBi
	\item $\lambda$ is the wavelength, set at 0.125m
	\item $r$ is the distance between the antennas, varying from 0.25m to 5m
\end{itemize}

\begin{comment}
	The term
	\begin{gather}
		\left(\frac{4\pi r}{\lambda}\right)^2
	\end{gather}
	represents the free-space loss.
\end{comment}
\begin{gather}
	P_r = 107.584 \cdot \left(\frac{0.125}{4\pi r}\right)^2 \nonumber
\end{gather}

For each distance, the received power is calculated using the equation~\ref{eq:friis}. \\
The resultant graph has been plotted in \ref{fig:xscreen9}.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Calculation of the free space loss during all measurements:}}

The term
\begin{gather}
	L_{fs} = \mathrm{20}\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi r}{\lambda}\right)	\label{eq:free_space}
\end{gather}
represents the free-space loss. \\Hence, the calculation of r is essential before calculation of the free space loss itself.

Rearranging Friis equation (cited from \cite{friis-script}) gives:
\begin{gather}
	P_r = P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r \cdot \left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi r}\right)^2 \nonumber \\
	r = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{P_t \cdot G_t \cdot G_r}{P_r}} \label{eq:friis_r}
\end{gather}

One can consider the horn antenna as the reference measurement for the Gain ($G_r$) at 1GHz. The maximum received power ($P_r$ for vertical polarization) is -21.803 dBm, in mW it becomes $6.602 \times 10^{-3}$. The gain of the measuring antenna is assumed to be 1. $G_t$ in linear units is $10^{7.9/10} = 6.166$.
\begin{gather}
	r = \frac{0.3}{4\pi}\sqrt{\frac{10 \cdot 6.166 \cdot 1}{6.602\times10^{-3}}} \nonumber \\
	\boxed{r = 2.307\mathrm{m}} \label{eq:antenna_dist}
\end{gather}

Using the value of \ref{eq:antenna_dist} in \ref{eq:free_space}, one can obtain:
\begin{gather}
	L_{\mathrm{fs-1GHz}} = \mathrm{20}\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot2.307}{0.30}\right) =  39.70\,\mathrm{dB} \nonumber \\
	L_{\mathrm{fs-2GHz}} = \mathrm{20}\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot2.307}{0.15}\right) = 45.72\,\mathrm{dB} \nonumber \\
	L_{\mathrm{fs-3GHz}} = \mathrm{20}\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot2.307}{0.10}\right) = 49.25\,\mathrm{dB} \nonumber \\
	L_{\mathrm{fs-2.4GHz}} = \mathrm{20}\log_{10}\left(\frac{4\pi \cdot2.307}{0.125}\right) = 47.31\,\mathrm{dB} \nonumber
\end{gather}

\subsection{Additional questions}
\textbf{Q. Is it possible to match all loads with an additional passive impedance matching
	network, consisting of transmission line and Capacitor?}\\
A. Analyzing this question from the perspective of individual loads and their ideal effects on a transmission line, stating once again that the line impedance is 50$\Omega$ and the objective is to match each load impedance to this value:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{50$\Omega$ load}: This load is already matched, nothing to do. \textbf{Possible}.
	\item \textbf{25$\Omega$ and 100$\Omega$ loads}: These are not matched, there are considerable reflections but it is rather simple to match them to the 50$\Omega$ load. \textbf{Possible}.
	\item \textbf{Open and short loads}: These loads represent extreme cases since both their reflection coefficient magnitudes are near 1, this cannot be matched only using a capacitor, one would require a real resistance.
	So, using only a transmission line and a capacitor, \textbf{it is not possible}.
	\item \textbf{Capacitive load}: A capacitive load falls at the outer circumference of the Smith chart. The capacitive part can be cancelled by an inductive reactance of the line, but the same phenomenon seen with open and short arises, it has no resistive part, and this cannot be compensated using only a simple passive network described here, hence, it is \textbf{not possible}.
\end{itemize} 

\textbf{Q. Calculate an impedance matching network (by using a transmission line + capacitor)
	for a load given by the supervisor.}\\
A. Matching the measured impedance $Z_L = 105.4 - 3j$ to $Z_0 = 50\Omega$ includes the following steps:

It shall be matched to a transmission line with characteristic impedance
\[
Z_0 = 50~\Omega
\]
at the frequency
\[
f = 310~\mathrm{MHz}.
\]

The wavelength is
\[
\lambda = 96.6667~\mathrm{cm} = 0.966667~\mathrm{m}.
\]

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Normalize the load impedance:
	\[
	z_L = \frac{Z_L}{Z_0}
	= \frac{105.4-j3}{50}
	= 2.108-j0.06.
	\]
	
	\item A 50$\Omega$ transmission line transforms the normalized load
	impedance according to \cite{script}:
	\[
	z_\mathrm{in} =
	\frac{z_L+j\tan(\beta l)}
	{1+jz_L\tan(\beta l)}
	\]
	where
	\[
	\beta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}=6.5
	\]
	
	\item The line length is selected such that the real part of the
	normalized input admittance is equal to one:
	\[
	y_\mathrm{in}=\frac{1}{z_\mathrm{in}}=1-j0.7643
	\]
	
	This condition is obtained for:
	\[
	l=0.3432\cdot\lambda
	\]
	
	Using the given wavelength:
	\[
	l=0.3432\cdot 0.966667
	=0.3317~\mathrm{m}
	\]
	
	Therefore, the required transmission-line length is:
	\[
	\boxed{l\approx33.17~\mathrm{cm}}
	\]
	
	\item At this position, we calculate:
	\[
	Y_\mathrm{in}
	=\frac{1-j0.7643}{50}
	=0.02-j0.01529
	\]
	
	The capacitor is connected in parallel with the input of the line.
	It must provide the opposite susceptance:
	\[
	B_C=+0.01529
	\]
	
	Since
	\[
	B_C=2\pi fC,
	\]
	the required capacitance is:
	\[
	C=\frac{0.01529}{2\pi\cdot310\cdot10^6}
	\]
	
	Thus:
	\[
	\boxed{C\approx7.85~\mathrm{pF}}
	\]
	
	\item Finally the total input admittance after adding the capacitor comes out to be:
	\[
	Y_\mathrm{total}
	=(0.02-j0.01529)+j0.01529
	=0.02
	\]
	
	Therefore:
	\[
	Z_\mathrm{total}=\frac{1}{Y_\mathrm{total}}
	=\frac{1}{0.02}
	=50~\Omega
	\]
\end{enumerate}

Thus, the characteristics of a matched load at 310 MHz using a 50$\Omega$ transmission line
is with a length of 33.17 cm and a shunt capacitor of approximately 7.85 pF.
\end{comment}

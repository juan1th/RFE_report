\subsubsection{Short discussion of measurement results}

Considering the measured transmission and reflection parameters, the distinct characteristics of the three filters can be clearly identified:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lowpass Filter (SLP-100):} The magnitude plot exhibits a clear low-pass characteristic, passing frequencies below approximately 100 MHz. In the stopband at the 2.025 GHz marker, the transmission ($S_{21}$) is highly attenuated (-56.11 dB), while the reflection ($S_{11}$) is nearly 0 dB (-0.26 dB). This indicates that in the stopband, the rejected signal is almost entirely reflected rather than absorbed. The Smith chart confirms this, as the impedance trace moves tightly along the highly reactive outer boundary for stopband frequencies.
    
    \item \textbf{Highpass Filter (SHP-100):} This filter demonstrates a high-pass response, heavily attenuating low frequencies but allowing frequencies above $\sim$100 MHz to pass. At the 2.025 GHz marker (which falls within the passband), the transmission is excellent with negligible insertion loss ($S_{21}$ = -0.29 dB) and the reflection is minimized ($S_{11}$ = -26.96 dB). On the Smith chart, the passband frequencies converge securely near the 50 $\Omega$ center matching point.
    
    \item \textbf{Bandpass Filter (SIF-70):} The magnitude plot reveals a distinct passband centered near 70 MHz. At higher frequencies outside this band, such as the 2.025 GHz marker, the signal transmission is attenuated ($S_{21}$ = -13.33 dB) and the reflection increases accordingly ($S_{11}$ = -2.04 dB).
\end{itemize}

Regarding the order of the filters, it can be estimated by observing the attenuation slope in the stopband of the logarithmic magnitude plots. The extreme steepness of the transition from the passband to the stopband - particularly visible as a sharp, near-vertical drop in the SLP-100 and SHP-100 plots—indicates a high roll-off rate per decade. This suggests that these components are higher-order filters, which is necessary to provide such a rapid cut-off.

Furthermore, the transmission phase plots confirm the passband and stopband regions of the filters. Within the passbands, the phase shifts uniformly over frequency. In the stopband regions, the deep attenuation drops the signal near the network analyser's noise floor, which results in the rapid, chaotic phase variations visible in the diagrams.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

To calculate the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) from the measured logarithmic reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$ in decibels) for the measurement table, the following formula is applied:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 10^{\frac{S_{11}}{20}}}{1 - 10^{\frac{S_{11}}{20}}}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the "Calculated" column of the measurement table, Line 4 (Red Trace) is evaluated below. Given the measured reflection coefficient of $S_{11} = -14.3692$ dB, the VSWR is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 10^{\frac{-14.3692}{20}}}{1 - 10^{\frac{-14.3692}{20}}} = \frac{1 + 0.1912}{1 - 0.1912} = 1.47
\end{equation}

\subsection{3-Port Circulator}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The objective of this task is to measure the complete S-parameter matrix of a 3-port circulator using a network analyser and compare the measured characteristics against the ideal theoretical behaviour of a circulator. Two different physical components were measured: a larger circulator optimized for the $\sim$900 MHz band, and a smaller circulator optimized for the $\sim$2.4 GHz band.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
Below is the block diagram for the 3-port circulator measurement setup. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth, height=0.25\textheight, keepaspectratio]{src/figers/ex_3/3_port_circulator.png}
    \caption{Block diagram of the 3-port circulator with measuring points clearly marked for ports 1, 2, and 3.}
    \label{fig:circulator_schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}
A circulator is an asymmetric, non-reciprocal component. According to the laboratory manual, the ideal scattering matrix (S-matrix) of a perfectly matched, lossless 3-port circulator is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{ideal}} = \begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Network analysers display the measured transmission and reflection coefficients in decibels (dB). To directly compare the measured results with the ideal linear voltage ratios shown in the theoretical matrix, the measured dB values must be converted back to linear magnitudes using the calculation: 

\begin{equation}
    |S_{\text{linear}}| = 10^{\frac{S_{\text{dB}}}{20}}
\end{equation}

The linear S-matrix for the 901 MHz circulator is reconstructed from the calculated magnitudes in Table 3.3:

\begin{equation}
    S = \begin{bmatrix}
        0.0160 & 0.0230 & 0.9781 \\
        0.9777 & 0.0231 & 0.0205 \\
        0.0295 & 0.9773 & 0.0122
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Comparing this to the ideal theoretical matrix confirms the expected characteristics of the circulator:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Impedance Matching:} The main diagonal entries ($S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, $S_{33}$) are near zero (below 0.025 linear). This indicates excellent impedance matching at all ports, achieving return losses better than 32 dB.
    
    \item \textbf{Forward Transmission:} The forward parameters ($S_{21}$, $S_{32}$, $S_{13}$) are approximately 0.98. This confirms the non-reciprocal behaviour of the component, as power flows strictly in a clockwise circle (port 1 to 2, port 2 to 3, and port 3 to 1).

    \item \textbf{Frequency Dependency:} The measurements verify that circulators are strictly frequency-dependent due to the physical size of their ferrite cores. The larger circulator is optimized for a longer wavelength (901 MHz), while the smaller circulator operates at a shorter wavelength (2.436 GHz).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The task is to measure the S-parameters of a 3-port resistive power divider using a network analyser. The objective is to verify that the power divider matches all three ports to the characteristic impedance and exhibits a total loss of 6 dB. Furthermore, the measurement should determine whether there is any isolation between output ports 2 and 3.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
Below is the circuit diagram for the 3-port resistive power divider.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

A completely matched, reciprocal 3-port network cannot be lossless. A resistive power divider uses internal resistors to match all three ports simultaneously to the characteristic impedance ($Z_0$). Because of the reciprocal and symmetrical nature of this specific resistive network, the theoretical linear S-parameter matrix is expected to be:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{ideal}} = 0.5 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        0 & 1 & 1 \\
        1 & 0 & 1 \\
        1 & 1 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Network analysers display these values in decibels (dB). The theoretical forward transmission linear magnitude of 0.5 corresponds to the following theoretical loss:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{dB}} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(0.5) = -6.02 \text{ dB}
\end{equation}

To directly compare the measured decibel results from the network analyser with the ideal linear voltage ratios, the measured dB values must be converted back to linear magnitudes using the following calculation:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{\text{linear}}| = 10^{\frac{S_{\text{dB}}}{20}}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the "Calculated" column of the measurement table, the forward transmission parameter ($S_{21}$) is evaluated below. Given the measured transmission coefficient of $S_{21} = -6.0411$ dB, the linear magnitude is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{21}| = 10^{\frac{-6.0411}{20}} = 0.4988
\end{equation}

Based on the measurement data, the operational characteristics of the resistive power divider are clearly verified:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Impedance Matching:} The reflection coefficients ($S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, $S_{33}$) are all below -33 dB (equivalent to a linear magnitude of less than 0.022). The Smith chart validates this, as the traces contract securely into the center matching point (e.g., $S_{11}$ reads $52.17\ \Omega - j0.29\ \Omega$).

This lack of isolation is the defining difference between a basic resistive power divider and a Wilkinson power divider, which actively isolates its outputs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Antenna Reflection Coefficients}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The task is to measure the reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$) of different antennas using a network analyser. Based on the measurements, the reflection characteristics of two different antenna configurations (Antenna 1 and Antenna 2 with a ground plane) are evaluated. The measurement results must be displayed both as a Smith chart and as a logarithmic magnitude plot over frequency.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
The antenna under test is connected directly to Port 1 of the calibrated VNA. Measurements are recorded for Antenna 1 (unmatched monopole/dipole) and Antenna 2 (mounted on a metallic ground plane).

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The reflection factor $\rho$ (which corresponds to the $S_{11}$ parameter measured by the network analyser) at the point of the load is defined as the ratio of the backward to the forward voltage wave. It relates the antenna's input impedance ($Z_L$) to the characteristic impedance of the measurement system ($Z_w$, typically 50 $\Omega$):

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{Z_L - Z_w}{Z_L + Z_w}
\end{equation}

The network analyser displays the logarithmic magnitude of the reflection coefficient in decibels, calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{11,\text{dB}} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(|S_{11}|)
\end{equation}

To assess the matching quality, the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) is calculated from the linear reflection magnitude $|S_{11}|$:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |S_{11}|}{1 - |S_{11}|}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the "Calculated" column of the measurement table, Antenna 1 is evaluated below. Given the measured reflection coefficient of $S_{11} = -9.6100$ dB, the linear magnitude is:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{11}| = 10^{\frac{-9.6100}{20}} = 0.3307
\end{equation}

The VSWR is then calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 0.3307}{1 - 0.3307} = 1.99
\end{equation}

\subsection{Unmatched Antenna and Matching Network}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The objective of this task is to measure the reflection coefficient of an unmatched antenna at a frequency of 2.4 GHz. Based on this measurement, an impedance matching network consisting of a transmission line and a series capacitor must be calculated to match the antenna to the 50 $\Omega$ system. The solution is derived using both an analytical calculation and a graphical solution via the Smith chart. Finally, the calculated matching network is verified to observe the final matched reflection coefficient.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
A specific circuit diagram is not required for the initial measurement, as the unmatched antenna is connected directly to the calibrated port of the network analyser. The equivalent circuit of the calculated matching network consists of the antenna ($Z_A$) connected to a transmission line of length $l_e$, followed by a series capacitor $C$ connecting to the 50 $\Omega$ measurement port.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The unmatched antenna presents a load impedance $Z_A$ which can be determined from the measured reflection coefficient magnitude and phase. The normalized load impedance is defined as $z_A = \frac{Z_A}{Z_w}$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[a)] \textbf{Analytical Solution:} To match the antenna using a lossless transmission line (length $l_e$) and a series reactive element ($j x_s$), the normalized reactance $x_s$ is calculated via:
    
    \begin{equation}
        x_s = \pm \frac{2 \cdot |\rho_A|}{\sqrt{1 - |\rho_A|^2}}
    \end{equation}
    
    Because the matching network specifically requires a series capacitor, the negative solution for $x_s$ must be chosen. The actual capacitance $C$ is then calculated from the denormalized reactance $X_s = Z_w \cdot x_s$:
    
    \begin{equation}
        C = \frac{-1}{2 \cdot \pi \cdot f \cdot X_s}
    \end{equation}
    
    \textbf{Exemplary Analytical Calculation:} Given the measured unmatched reflection coefficient magnitude of $|\rho_A| = 0.5424$ (converted from $-5.32$ dB), the normalized series reactance is calculated as:
    
    \begin{equation}
        x_s = -\frac{2 \cdot 0.5424}{\sqrt{1 - 0.5424^2}} = -1.291
    \end{equation}
    
    Denormalizing this to a $50\ \Omega$ system yields $X_s = -64.55\ \Omega$. The required series capacitance at $f = 2.4$ GHz is then:
    
    \begin{equation}
        C = \frac{-1}{2 \cdot \pi \cdot 2.4 \times 10^9 \cdot (-64.55)} = 1.03 \text{ pF}
    \end{equation}

    \item[b)] \textbf{Smith Chart Solution:} In the Smith chart, the initial normalized impedance $z_A$ is plotted. Moving along the transmission line corresponds to rotating clockwise (towards the generator) along the constant $|\rho_A|$ circle. The rotation stops when this circle intersects the matching circle (the $r = 1$ circle). The distance rotated along the outer wavelength scale determines the required line length $l_e$. At this intersection, the impedance has the form $1 + jx$. To achieve perfect matching, a series capacitor providing a normalized reactance of $-jx$ must be added to move the point to the centre of the Smith chart ($1 + j0$).
    
    \textbf{Exemplary Graphical Steps:} Plotting the initial normalized unmatched impedance ($z_A = 2.275 - j1.473$) and rotating clockwise along the constant $|\rho_A| = 0.5424$ circle yields an intersection with the $r = 1$ matching circle exactly at the normalized impedance coordinate of $1 + j1.291$. Moving downward along the $r = 1$ circle by subtracting the calculated capacitive reactance ($-j1.291$) pulls the impedance directly into the ideal $1 + j0$ center matching point.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

A mechanical slide-screw tuner consists of a $50\ \Omega$ slabline and a movable capacitive/reflective probe (the slug). The impedance matching is achieved mechanically rather than through fixed lumped components. To quantify the matching improvement, the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) is calculated from the measured reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$) in decibels.

First, the linear magnitude is calculated:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{11}| = 10^{\frac{S_{11,\text{dB}}}{20}}
\end{equation}

Then, the VSWR is determined:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |S_{11}|}{1 - |S_{11}|}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation: To demonstrate the calculation process, the final matched state using the slide-screw tuner is evaluated below. Given the measured reflection coefficient of $S_{11} = -40.4259$ dB, the linear magnitude is:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{11}| = 10^{\frac{-40.4259}{20}} = 0.0095
\end{equation}

The VSWR is then calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 0.0095}{1 - 0.0095} = 1.019 \text{ (rounded to 1.02)}
\end{equation}

Based on the measurement data, the mechanical impedance matching process was highly successful:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial Unmatched State:} The antenna initially exhibited a high reflection coefficient of $-5.27$ dB at $2.4$ GHz, corresponding to a poor VSWR of $3.40$. This indicated a severe impedance mismatch where a significant portion of the transmitted power was being reflected.

The marker reading of $49.078\ \Omega + j2.953\ \Omega$ confirms that the reactive components of the unmatched antenna were effectively cancelled out.

This practically demonstrates that the mechanical slide-screw tuner successfully matched the antenna to the $50\ \Omega$ system without the need to calculate or solder fixed physical capacitors or transmission line segments.
\end{itemize}

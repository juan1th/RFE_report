\section{S-Parameters}
\subsection{Coaxial Filters}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The network analyzer was calibrated (transmission and reflection for all 4 ports) to measure the 2-port parameters of three coaxial filters: a Lowpass (SLP-100), a Highpass (SHP-100), and a Bandpass (SIF-70). From these measurements, the characteristics and order of each filter were determined. Reflection coefficients were plotted on a Smith chart, and transmission parameters were recorded as logarithmic magnitude and phase.

\subsubsection{Schematic}

A specific circuit diagram is not required for this task, as the measurement simply involves connecting the coaxial filters directly between the calibrated ports of the network analyser.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The order of a filter ($n$) can be determined by evaluating the steep roll-off slope in the stopband of the transmission parameter ($S_{21}$). A standard filter rolls off at $n \times 20$ dB/decade. The slope is calculated using:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Slope} = \frac{\Delta S_{21}}{\Delta \log_{10}(f)}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Lowpass Filter (SLP-100) Calculation:}
Extracting two points from the logarithmic $S_{21}$ plot ($f_1 = 100$ MHz at $-3$ dB, and $f_2 = 200$ MHz at $-50$ dB):

\begin{equation}
    \text{Slope} = \frac{-50\text{ dB} - (-3\text{ dB})}{\log_{10}(200\text{ MHz}) - \log_{10}(100\text{ MHz})} = \frac{-47\text{ dB}}{\log_{10}(2)} \approx -156\text{ dB/decade}
\end{equation}

Dividing this slope by the standard $20$ dB/decade roll-off per pole yields $n = 156 / 20 = 7.8$, confirming an 8th-order filter.

\paragraph{Highpass Filter (SHP-100) Calculation:}
Extracting two points from the attenuation slope below the cutoff frequency ($f_1 = 100$ MHz at $-3$ dB, and $f_2 = 50$ MHz at $-50$ dB):

\begin{equation}
    \text{Slope} = \frac{-3\text{ dB} - (-50\text{ dB})}{\log_{10}(100\text{ MHz}) - \log_{10}(50\text{ MHz})} = \frac{47\text{ dB}}{\log_{10}(2)} \approx 156\text{ dB/decade}
\end{equation}

Dividing this by $20$ dB/decade proves this is also an 8th-order filter.

\subsubsection{Short discussion of measurement results}

Based on the transmission and reflection measurements, the characteristics of the three coaxial filters can be evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lowpass Filter (SLP-100):} Passes frequencies below $\sim\!100$ MHz. At the $2.025$ GHz stopband marker, transmission is highly attenuated ($S_{21} = -56.11$ dB), while reflection is nearly $0$ dB ($S_{11} = -0.26$ dB). The rejected signal is almost entirely reflected, which is confirmed by the Smith chart trace moving along the highly reactive outer boundary. As proven in the calculations, the steep $-156$ dB/decade roll-off confirms it is an 8th-order filter.
    \item \textbf{Highpass Filter (SHP-100):} Passes frequencies above $\sim\!100$ MHz. At the $2.025$ GHz passband marker, insertion loss is negligible ($S_{21} = -0.29$ dB) and reflection is minimized ($S_{11} = -26.96$ dB). Passband frequencies properly converge near the $50\,\Omega$ center of the Smith chart. The $156$ dB/decade roll-off proves this is also an 8th-order filter.
    \item \textbf{Bandpass Filter (SIF-70):} The passband is centered near $70$ MHz. At $2.025$ GHz, transmission is attenuated ($S_{21} = -13.33$ dB) and reflection increases ($S_{11} = -2.04$ dB).
    \item \textbf{Phase Characteristics:} The phase shifts uniformly within the passbands. In the stopbands, attenuation drops the signal near the network analyzer's noise floor, causing the chaotic phase variations visible in the plots.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

To calculate the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) from the measured logarithmic reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$ in decibels) for the measurement table, the following formula is applied:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 10^{\frac{S_{11}}{20}}}{1 - 10^{\frac{S_{11}}{20}}}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the "Calculated" column of the measurement table, Line 4 (Red Trace) is evaluated below. Given the measured reflection coefficient of $S_{11} = -14.3692$ dB, the VSWR is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 10^{\frac{-14.3692}{20}}}{1 - 10^{\frac{-14.3692}{20}}} = \frac{1 + 0.1912}{1 - 0.1912} = 1.47
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The complete S-parameter matrix of a 3-port circulator was measured using a network analyzer to compare its performance against ideal theoretical behavior. Measurements were performed on two distinct physical components: a larger circulator optimized for the $\sim$900 MHz band and a smaller circulator optimized for the $\sim$2.4 GHz band.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
Below is the block diagram for the 3-port circulator measurement setup. 

\begin{figure}[H]

    \caption{Block diagram of the 3-port circulator with measuring points clearly marked for ports 1, 2, and 3.}
    \label{fig:circulator_schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}
A circulator is an asymmetric, non-reciprocal component. According to the laboratory manual, the ideal scattering matrix (S-matrix) of a perfectly matched, lossless 3-port circulator is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{ideal}} = \begin{bmatrix}
    0 & 1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 1 \\
    1 & 0 & 0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Network analysers display the measured transmission and reflection coefficients in decibels (dB). To directly compare the measured results with the ideal linear voltage ratios shown in the theoretical matrix, the measured dB values must be converted back to linear magnitudes using the calculation: 

\begin{equation}
    |S_{\text{linear}}| = 10^{\frac{S_{\text{dB}}}{20}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{3-Port Resistive Power Divider}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The S-parameters of a 3-port resistive power divider were measured using a network analyzer. The measurements verified port matching to the characteristic impedance, evaluated the 6 dB total transmission loss, and determined whether isolation exists between output ports 2 and 3.

\subsubsection{Schematic}
Below is the circuit diagram for the 3-port resistive power divider.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth, height=0.4\textheight, keepaspectratio]{src/figers/ex_3/3_port_resistive_power_divider.png}
    \caption{Circuit diagram of the 3-port resistive power divider with measuring points clearly marked for ports 1, 2, and 3.}
    \label{fig:power_divider_schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

\textbf{Calculation of the Resistor Values:} A symmetric 3-port resistive power divider utilizes a star configuration of three identical internal resistors ($R$) to match all ports to the characteristic impedance ($Z_0 = 50\,\Omega$). When Port 2 and Port 3 are terminated with $Z_0$, the input impedance looking into Port 1 must also equal $Z_0$. The equivalent circuit looking into Port 1 consists of the first resistor $R$ in series with the parallel combination of the other two branches (each having a resistance of $R + Z_0$).

\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{in}} = R + \frac{R + Z_0}{2}
\end{equation}

To achieve perfect matching, we set $Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0$:

\begin{align}
    Z_0 &= \frac{3R + Z_0}{2} \\
    2Z_0 &= 3R + Z_0 \\
    R &= \frac{Z_0}{3}
\end{align}

For a standard $50\,\Omega$ measurement system, the required resistor values are:

\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{50\,\Omega}{3} = 16.67\,\Omega
\end{equation}

\textbf{Theoretical S-Parameters:} A completely matched, reciprocal 3-port network cannot be lossless. Because of the reciprocal and symmetrical nature of this specific resistive network, the theoretical linear S-parameter matrix is expected to be:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{ideal}} = 0.5 
    \begin{pmatrix} 
        0 & 1 & 1 \\ 
        1 & 0 & 1 \\ 
        1 & 1 & 0 
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Network analysers display these values in decibels (dB). The theoretical forward transmission linear magnitude of $0.5$ corresponds to the following theoretical loss:

\begin{equation}
    S_{\text{dB}} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(0.5) = -6.02\text{ dB}
\end{equation}

To directly compare the measured decibel results from the network analyser with the ideal linear voltage ratios, the measured dB values must be converted back to linear magnitudes using the following calculation:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{\text{linear}}| = 10^{(S_{\text{dB}} / 20)}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Exemplary Calculation:} To demonstrate the calculation process for the values populated in the ``Calculated'' column of the measurement table, the forward transmission parameter ($S_{21}$) is evaluated below. Given the measured transmission coefficient of $S_{21} = -6.0411$ dB, the linear magnitude is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{21}| = 10^{(-6.0411 / 20)} = 0.4988
\end{equation}

\subsection{Antenna Reflection Coefficients}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$) of various antennas was measured using a network analyzer. The reflection characteristics of two distinct antenna configurations, Antenna 1 and Antenna 2 mounted with a ground plane were evaluated. Results are presented using both logarithmic magnitude frequency plots and Smith chart representations.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The reflection factor $\rho$ (which corresponds to the $S_{11}$ parameter measured by the network analyser) at the point of the load is defined as the ratio of the backward to the forward voltage wave. It relates the antenna's input impedance ($Z_L$) to the characteristic impedance of the measurement system ($Z_w$, typically 50 $\Omega$):

\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{Z_L - Z_w}{Z_L + Z_w}
\end{equation}

The network analyser displays the logarithmic magnitude of the reflection coefficient in decibels, calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    S_{11,\text{dB}} = 20 \cdot \log_{10}(|S_{11}|)
\end{equation}

To assess the matching quality, the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) is calculated from the linear reflection magnitude $|S_{11}|$:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |S_{11}|}{1 - |S_{11}|}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation:

\subsection{Unmatched Antenna and Matching Network}

\subsubsection{Task Description}
The reflection coefficient of an unmatched antenna was measured at 2.4 GHz. Using both analytical calculations and a graphical Smith chart method, an L-section matching network consisting of a transmission line and a series capacitor was designed to match the antenna impedance to the 50 $\Omega$ system. The designed matching network was then physically implemented and verified to record the improved reflection coefficient.

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

The unmatched antenna presents a load impedance $Z_A$ which can be determined from the measured reflection coefficient magnitude and phase. The normalized load impedance is defined as $z_A = \frac{Z_A}{Z_w}$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[a)] \textbf{Analytical Solution:} To match the antenna using a lossless transmission line (length $l_e$) and a series reactive element ($j x_s$), the normalized reactance $x_s$ is calculated via:
    
    \begin{equation}
        x_s = \pm \frac{2 \cdot |\rho_A|}{\sqrt{1 - |\rho_A|^2}}
    \end{equation}
    
    Because the matching network specifically requires a series capacitor, the negative solution for $x_s$ must be chosen. The actual capacitance $C$ is then calculated from the denormalized reactance $X_s = Z_w \cdot x_s$:
    
    \begin{equation}
        C = \frac{-1}{2 \cdot \pi \cdot f \cdot X_s}
    \end{equation}
    
    \textbf{Exemplary Analytical Calculation:} Given the measured unmatched reflection coefficient magnitude of $|\rho_A| = 0.5424$ (converted from $-5.32$ dB), the normalized series reactance is calculated as:
    
    \begin{equation}
        x_s = -\frac{2 \cdot 0.5424}{\sqrt{1 - 0.5424^2}} = -1.291
    \end{equation}
    
    Denormalizing this to a $50\ \Omega$ system yields $X_s = -64.55\ \Omega$. The required series capacitance at $f = 2.4$ GHz is then:
    
    \begin{equation}
        C = \frac{-1}{2 \cdot \pi \cdot 2.4 \times 10^9 \cdot (-64.55)} = 1.03 \text{ pF}
    \end{equation}

    \item[b)] \textbf{Smith Chart Solution:} In the Smith chart, the initial normalized impedance $z_A$ is plotted. Moving along the transmission line corresponds to rotating clockwise (towards the generator) along the constant $|\rho_A|$ circle. The rotation stops when this circle intersects the matching circle (the $r = 1$ circle). The distance rotated along the outer wavelength scale determines the required line length $l_e$. At this intersection, the impedance has the form $1 + jx$. To achieve perfect matching, a series capacitor providing a normalized reactance of $-jx$ must be added to move the point to the centre of the Smith chart ($1 + j0$).
    
    \textbf{Exemplary Graphical Steps:} Plotting the initial normalized unmatched impedance ($z_A = 2.275 - j1.473$) and rotating clockwise along the constant $|\rho_A| = 0.5424$ circle yields an intersection with the $r = 1$ matching circle exactly at the normalized impedance coordinate of $1 + j1.291$. Moving downward along the $r = 1$ circle by subtracting the calculated capacitive reactance ($-j1.291$) pulls the impedance directly into the ideal $1 + j0$ center matching point.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Formulas and Calculations}

A mechanical slide-screw tuner consists of a $50\ \Omega$ slabline and a movable capacitive/reflective probe (the slug). The impedance matching is achieved mechanically rather than through fixed lumped components. To quantify the matching improvement, the Voltage Standing Wave Ratio (VSWR) is calculated from the measured reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$) in decibels.

First, the linear magnitude is calculated:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{11}| = 10^{\frac{S_{11,\text{dB}}}{20}}
\end{equation}

Then, the VSWR is determined:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + |S_{11}|}{1 - |S_{11}|}
\end{equation}

Exemplary Calculation: To demonstrate the calculation process, the final matched state using the slide-screw tuner is evaluated below. Given the measured reflection coefficient of $S_{11} = -40.4259$ dB, the linear magnitude is:

\begin{equation}
    |S_{11}| = 10^{\frac{-40.4259}{20}} = 0.0095
\end{equation}

The VSWR is then calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{VSWR} = \frac{1 + 0.0095}{1 - 0.0095} = 1.019 \text{ (rounded to 1.02)}
\end{equation}
